# How to Answer “Tell Me About Yourself” in an Interview

A focused introduction, two examples, and an exercise you can try out loud.

JobHackAISeptember 29, 2026

Start with the work you do now, choose one relevant example, and explain why this role interests you. That gives you a useful outline for “Tell me about yourself” without having to recite your resume.

Before you write anything, underline two responsibilities in the job description. Your introduction should help the interviewer see how your experience connects to that work.

## Build an answer from three points

1.  **Your current work.** Name your role or professional focus, then say what you handle. If you are between jobs, start with your most recent relevant experience. A student can use a project, internship or volunteer responsibility.
2.  **One piece of evidence.** Pick a responsibility, project or result you can explain in detail. Say what you personally did. Use a number only when you can support it; a clear description of your contribution also works.
3.  **Your connection to this role.** Choose something specific in the posting and explain why it fits the work you want to do next.

This present, relevant past, next-step outline follows a common approach described in [Indeed’s interview guide](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/interview-question-tell-me-about-yourself) and [The Muse’s answer guide](https://www.themuse.com/advice/tell-me-about-yourself-interview-question-answer-examples). Adjust the order when an earlier experience is the clearest connection to the job.

## Example: customer success

The examples below are fictional illustrations, not customer stories or scripts to copy. Replace every detail with your own experience.

> “I’m a customer success manager working with B2B software accounts. I handle onboarding and help customers get through the first steps of using the product.
>
> Before this, I worked in account support. That taught me to ask specific questions when a customer says something isn’t working, and to give the technical team enough detail to investigate.
>
> This role interests me because it combines onboarding with ongoing account planning. I’d like to build on the implementation work I already do and take more responsibility for what happens after launch.”

This answer names the work, shows a useful skill from an earlier role, and makes the reason for applying concrete. It also leaves room for a follow-up about an actual onboarding project.

## Example: moving from project coordination to project management

> “I’m a project coordinator at a healthcare technology company. I keep launch schedules up to date, follow up on blockers, and make sure product, engineering and customer teams have the information they need.
>
> One part I’ve taken on is documenting decisions after our planning meetings, including who owns the next step. It gives the team a shared record when priorities change.
>
> I’m applying for this project manager role because I want to take more ownership of planning and risk. The cross-team delivery work in the description is closely related to what I already support.”

The example describes a plausible step in responsibility without pretending the candidate already owns the whole project. In your version, be equally clear about what you led, supported or learned.

## Try one short practice round

1.  **Write three prompts.** Use a few words for your current work, one example, and your reason for applying.
2.  **Answer out loud.** For this exercise, try a 45 to 75 second answer, then adjust it to the interviewer and the question.
3.  **Check the substance.** Could someone identify what you do, one relevant contribution, and why this job fits? Add the missing detail.
4.  **Try a follow-up.** Ask yourself, “What did you personally do in that project?” Answer using the same real example.
5.  **Repeat from the prompts.** Let the wording change. Keep the facts consistent.

If your answer runs long, cut the background that does not help explain your fit. If it feels vague, replace a phrase such as “strong communicator” with one thing you actually do, such as writing a clear handoff or resolving a customer’s question.
